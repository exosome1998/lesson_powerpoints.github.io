Marsden High School · Science 2027L05 · 10
Reading passage

READ · Weather forecasts (1 of 2)

Weather forecasts begin with observations. Weather stations measure temperature, air pressure, humidity, wind and rainfall near the ground. Radar sends radio waves and detects reflections from precipitation, helping scientists locate areas of rain. Satellites observe cloud patterns and other atmospheric features over large regions. Each instrument provides a different part of the picture.

Computers combine these observations with equations that describe atmospheric processes. A weather model divides the atmosphere into a grid and estimates how conditions change over a series of time steps. The model is a simplified representation: it cannot capture every small cloud or every movement of air. Scientists use its output to estimate possible future weather.

Marsden High School · Science 2027L05 · 11
Reading passage

READ · Weather forecasts (2 of 2)

A forecast is not a guarantee. The atmosphere is complex, measurements are incomplete and small differences in the starting conditions can influence later predictions. Different models may use different assumptions or grid sizes. Forecasters compare several sources of evidence and communicate uncertainty when conditions are difficult to predict.

After a forecast, scientists compare it with later observations. They check where predictions were close and where they differed. As new observations replace older estimates, updated model calculations may lead to a revised forecast. This cycle of observing, modelling, predicting and checking helps scientists improve their understanding of weather and make more useful decisions.
