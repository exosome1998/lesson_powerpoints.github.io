Changing models of the solar system

Learning intention: Use observations and the history of astronomy to explain why scientific models change.

R.E.A.D. · Changing solar system models

Read for the main idea. Circle scientific terms, underline evidence, then answer the questions. This is the unit’s one designated READ task.

Long ago, many people used an Earth-centred model to explain the sky. In the second century CE, Ptolemy described a detailed geocentric model. In this model, Earth was at the centre and the Sun, Moon and planets moved around it. The model helped people predict positions in the sky.

In 1543, Nicolaus Copernicus published a heliocentric model. He placed the Sun near the centre of the planetary system and described Earth as one of the planets orbiting the Sun. Earth's daily rotation could explain the apparent daily movement of the sky.

In 1610, Galileo used a telescope to observe Jupiter. He saw small points of light whose positions changed over several nights. He concluded that they were moons orbiting Jupiter. This observation challenged the claim that every object in the sky orbited Earth. Other observations, including the phases of Venus, also supported changes to earlier models.

Galileo's evidence did not establish every detail of the modern model by itself. Later scientists collected more observations and improved explanations of planetary motion. The example shows how scientific models are tested, revised and sometimes replaced when new evidence becomes available.
