READ literacy task: the evolution of atomic models

Learning intention: Explain how Dalton, Thomson, Rutherford and Bohr models changed with evidence.

READ · The evolution of atomic models

Read for the main idea. Circle key terms, underline evidence and use the text in your answers.

Scientists use models to represent objects that are too small to see directly. An atomic model is not a photograph of an atom. It is a representation that helps explain observations.

In the early nineteenth century, John Dalton described matter as made of tiny atoms. His model treated atoms as solid particles. Later, experiments with cathode rays provided evidence for negatively charged electrons. J. J. Thomson proposed a model in which electrons were embedded in positively charged matter.

In the early twentieth century, Ernest Rutherford and colleagues studied how positively charged particles travelled towards thin gold foil. Most passed through, but a small number were deflected through large angles. This evidence suggested that most of the atom was empty space, with much of its mass and positive charge concentrated in a small nucleus.

Niels Bohr proposed a model in which electrons could occupy particular energy levels. This helped explain the pattern of spectral lines from hydrogen. The simple shell diagrams used in school are based on this idea, but do not show the exact motion or position of electrons.

Each model was useful in its time. When new evidence challenged an earlier model, scientists developed a better representation. Modern atomic models are more detailed, and scientists continue to test explanations against evidence.
